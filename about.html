How it works

Aircraft broadcast their GPS position several times a second, together with numbers that say how much their own navigation system trusts that position. Volunteers with receivers collect these broadcasts, and adsb.lol publishes them openly. When a jammer drowns out the satellite signal, many aircraft in one area report low trust at the same time. When a spoofer sends a false signal, their reported positions start to move in ways their own speed does not allow.

Every night this site processes the previous day: about 4 GB of flight data, streamed and never stored. It cuts five study regions into 1° cells and 15-minute windows and asks two questions about each one: were aircraft here jammed, and were they spoofed?

What decides a cell

A machine-learning model (two gradient-boosted classifiers) reads the evidence in each cell, in its neighbouring cells and in the windows before and after: how many aircraft reported degraded GPS, how many fell back to ground-based positioning, how many showed impossible jumps or a mismatch between position change and reported ground speed, and how GPS altitude compares with barometric altitude. It never sees the location or the time of day, so it cannot simply learn that some places are usually jammed.

No labelled dataset of GPS interference exists. The model was trained on labels from seven hand-written rules, combined statistically, and on simulated attacks injected into real traffic, where the truth is known exactly. A cell needs at least three aircraft to be judged normal or counted in the shares. With one or two, a jamming or spoofing flag is still shown, dashed, because such flags were right 47 of 47 times on a hand-labelled test day; anything else is shown as low coverage.

For a group of jammed cells, the site estimates where a ground jammer would have to be to explain which aircraft were affected and which were not: a jammer reaches higher aircraft from farther away, because of the curvature of the Earth. The estimate is shown as a circle that should contain the source 9 times in 10, and only when the fit is good and its centre lies inside the area where aircraft are collected (outside it, nothing can confirm or rule out a jammer). This layer is experimental: it was tested on simulated jammers that follow the same physics as the fit, and its accuracy on real jammers is not known. The affected areas are the main result.

The numbers behind each cell, p_jamming and p_spoofing, are model scores. They are calibrated against the system's own training labels, so 0.8 means "8 in 10 such cells carried those labels in testing", not a validated 80% chance of real interference.

Known limits

  • On real data the model agrees with the rules almost everywhere. Its advantage at the weak edges of jammed areas is shown on simulated attacks only.
  • Spoofing is never independently confirmed, and over busy hotspots such as the Black Sea slow spoofing is often missed.
  • Flags on cells with one or two aircraft were checked on one day, almost all in the Baltic. When such a cell is not flagged, that says little, so it is shown as low coverage.
  • Both the model and the rules miss weak interference at the edges of hotspots: on 1 November an estimated 4-13% of judged cells, mostly over the Black Sea, were affected but shown as normal. The shares are therefore lower bounds.
  • Coverage changes when volunteers add or remove receivers. A region can look busier or quieter without any change in interference.
  • Several jammers close together are fitted as one. Jammer circles are statistical estimates, not attributions to any party.
  • The hand labels come from one person on two days.

What the site does not publish

No aircraft identifiers, callsigns or tracks: only counts per cell, and not even exact counts for cells with fewer than three aircraft. Days or regions whose input fails the quality checks (incomplete download, too little traffic or coverage) are withheld, with the reason. If the calm control region shows an unusual amount of interference, the day is still published but marked for review, so a real event is not hidden.
